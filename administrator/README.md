# When an Administrator Is Not Really an Administrator

Article II, Section 7, §5 requires Treasury Withdrawal actions to designate one or more administrators responsible for monitoring how the funds are used and ensuring that deliverables are achieved.

This requirement should be understood as a real governance function, not just a label.

The wording does not expressly state that an administrator must be independent from the recipient. It also does not expressly state that a recipient may act as its own sole administrator. The text is neutral on that specific question.

Because of that, the better interpretation comes from the purpose of the provision.

The administrator requirement exists to create meaningful oversight over treasury funds. It is meant to ensure that someone is responsible for monitoring the use of public funds and checking that the stated deliverables are achieved.

If the recipient is also the sole administrator, that function becomes circular. The same party receives the funds, controls the funds, monitors its own use of the funds, and confirms whether its own deliverables are achieved.

That is not meaningful administration.

It is self-monitoring.

This does not mean self-administration is always impossible. But if a proposal relies on self-administration, it should explain how the administrator role remains real despite the lack of separation.

Proper oversight does not require an oversight body to take over day-to-day management. It does not need to become the vendor, and it does not necessarily need unilateral control over the funds. But it must create real checks and balances around the administrator’s actions.

A credible structure can include:

- written legal contracts
- on-chain vendor contracts
- milestone-based delivery
- milestone attestations
- smart contract escrow
- public dashboards
- independent assurance
- timeouts that return unused or locked funds to the Treasury

It can also include permissioned actions where key steps cannot be taken by one party alone.

Funding a vendor contract, modifying a contract, dispersing funds, or reorganizing treasury UTxOs can require multiple signatures, including external oversight. This helps ensure that the administrator cannot act unilaterally on the most important actions.

That is the key distinction.

Oversight does not need to manage the proposal. It needs to check that administrative actions are consistent with the governance action approved by DReps, that vendor contracts match the approved scope, that modifications are explained and consented to where required, and that milestone payments are transparent and auditable.

This gives the administrator role substance.

The administrator retains responsibility, but the system creates external checks, on-chain visibility, and a process for verifying that funds move according to the approved purpose.

By contrast, if the recipient simply names itself as administrator and controls the funds directly, with no independent control layer, no meaningful milestone process, no external assurance, no escrow structure, and no clear oversight process, the administrator role risks becoming a formality.

The proposal may satisfy the word “administrator” while failing the purpose of Article II, Section 7, §5.

The Cardano Treasury should not rely on self-certification.

The administrator requirement should mean real administration.

If the only person watching the cookie jar is the one eating the cookies, we do not have oversight.

We have crumbs.
