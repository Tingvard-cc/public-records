## Constitutional Change Does Not Happen in a Vacuum

When people talk about updating a constitution, most of the attention goes to the text itself. The words, the thresholds, the new requirements. What often gets far less attention is how the system is supposed to move from the old rules to the new ones. That gap is where problems start.

A new constitution does not land in a vacuum. It lands in the middle of an active governance system, with proposals already submitted, votes already cast, and expectations already set. If you do not think about transition rules, you are effectively changing the rules of the game while the game is still being played.

## Transition Is a Political Decision, Not a Legal One

This is not just a legal question. Deciding whether to allow a transition period, whether to grandfather existing actions, or whether to enforce a hard cutover is a political decision. Those choices are about fairness, impact, and practical consequences.

That is why they belong with the DReps through collective decision making, not with the Constitutional Committee. The CC can only judge what is in front of them. They cannot invent transition rules after the fact.

## The Consequences We Are Seeing Now

We are now seeing the consequences of skipping that step. Active governance actions can suddenly become unconstitutional overnight, even if they were fully constitutional when they were submitted. That puts voters, proposers, and reviewers in an impossible position.

A clear example is the DeFi Liquidity Treasury Withdrawal. Regardless of whether it complied with the constitution at the time it entered the process, it no longer complies with the new constitution. Under the new rules, it is simply unconstitutional.

This creates a real conflict. Voters may have supported the action in good faith. Proposers followed the rules as they existed. And yet the action is invalidated because the constitutional baseline moved.

## This Was Avoidable

None of this needed to happen. The problem is not that the constitution changed, but that the change came without guidance on how ongoing actions should be treated.

This could have been handled cleanly by adding transition guidance directly to governance action metadata. Stating which constitution applies, whether new requirements apply retroactively, or whether a grace period exists would have made the outcome predictable.

Instead, the system is left to deal with uncertainty after the fact. That is not a failure of individuals or committees. It is a failure to treat transition as a first class concern when changing the constitution.
